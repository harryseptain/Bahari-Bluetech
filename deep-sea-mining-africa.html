Deep Sea Mining and
Africa's Coastal Communities:
What You Need to Know

Global interest in seabed minerals is accelerating fast. Here is what every African coastal community, maritime professional, and policymaker needs to understand — before the rules are written without us.

Key takeaway
Deep sea mining is still developing — but the governance rules, economic frameworks, and technical institutions being built right now will define whether Africa benefits or is bypassed. The time to engage is before the decisions are made, not after.

For generations, the ocean has supported millions of Africans through fishing, trade, transport, tourism, and culture. Along the coasts of Kenya, Tanzania, Mozambique, Madagascar, Seychelles, and South Africa, coastal communities have depended on the sea for food, employment, and daily life. Today, however, global attention is increasingly moving beyond the sea surface and toward the deep ocean floor — where valuable minerals needed for modern technology are believed to exist in enormous quantities.

This growing interest is known as deep sea mining. Around the world, governments, scientists, companies, and international organisations are discussing whether minerals from the deep ocean could help support the global transition toward cleaner energy and advanced technology. Yet for many coastal communities across Africa, terms such as "polymetallic nodules," "International Seabed Authority," and "Common Heritage of Mankind" remain unfamiliar and difficult to navigate.

As discussions about the future of ocean resources accelerate, it is essential that African coastal communities, maritime professionals, and policymakers understand what deep sea mining is, why it matters, what concerns exist, and how Africa can position itself to benefit — rather than simply observe — from the emerging seabed economy.

What Is Deep Sea Mining?

Deep sea mining refers to the process of extracting valuable minerals from the ocean floor — sometimes at depths exceeding 4,000 metres below the sea surface. These minerals occur in several geological forms and contain metals that are critical for modern industrial systems.

The most discussed deep sea mineral resource is polymetallic nodules — rock-like deposits found lying on the seabed that contain significant concentrations of nickel, cobalt, copper, and manganese. These metals are essential components of:

  • Electric vehicle batteries and energy storage systems
  • Renewable energy infrastructure — wind turbines, solar systems
  • Smartphones, computers, and digital devices
  • Industrial equipment and advanced manufacturing
Polymetallic nodules lying on the deep ocean seabed. These rock-like formations contain cobalt, nickel, copper, and manganese — minerals critical to modern clean energy technologies.

As the world accelerates its shift toward cleaner energy and reduced carbon emissions, demand for these minerals is growing rapidly. Because of this, some governments and companies are exploring whether the deep ocean could become a significant future source of critical raw materials.

⚠️
Important context: Deep sea mining is still a developing industry. Commercial-scale mining in international waters has not yet begun, and scientists are still actively studying the environmental effects that such activities could have on deep ocean ecosystems. The regulatory framework is being finalised now — which is precisely why engagement today matters.

Why Is the World Interested in Deep Ocean Minerals?

The modern world is becoming increasingly dependent on technologies that require large quantities of specific minerals. Electric vehicles, battery storage systems, solar energy infrastructure, and digital technologies all depend on metals that are in high and growing demand globally.

The global energy transition — from fossil fuels to electric vehicles, solar, and battery storage — is driving unprecedented demand for the minerals found in deep ocean seabeds.

Many governments are investing heavily in the global energy transition — the movement away from fossil fuels toward cleaner energy systems. As demand for clean energy technologies increases, so does the demand for the metals used to manufacture them. Currently, most of these minerals are mined on land. However, land-based mining has raised serious concerns related to environmental destruction, deforestation, pollution, unsafe working conditions, and social conflict in some regions.

Because of this, some researchers and industries believe that deep sea minerals could become an alternative — or complementary — source of critical materials in the coming decades. Supporters argue that ocean minerals may help support global clean energy goals. Others, however, believe that the environmental risks of disturbing deep ocean ecosystems are not yet sufficiently understood to justify commercial operations.

What Is the International Seabed Authority (ISA)?

The International Seabed Authority (ISA) is the international organisation responsible for regulating mineral-related activities in areas of the deep seabed that lie beyond national borders — what international law calls "the Area." It was established under the United Nations Convention on the Law of the Sea (UNCLOS).

One important principle behind the ISA is the concept that the deep seabed beyond national jurisdiction is the "Common Heritage of Mankind." Under international law, this means these ocean resources should be managed for the benefit of all humanity — not only for a few powerful countries or corporations.

"The deep seabed and its resources are the Common Heritage of Mankind. This is not a slogan — it is a legally binding principle under UNCLOS that obligates the ISA to ensure benefits flow to all nations, including developing states in Africa."

UNCLOS Article 136 — United Nations Convention on the Law of the Sea

The ISA is responsible for developing rules and regulations for deep sea mining, issuing exploration contracts, promoting scientific research, establishing environmental protection measures, and working to ensure that benefits from seabed resources are shared fairly across member states. Currently, the ISA has issued exploration licences permitting scientific surveys. Discussions about commercial-scale mining regulations — the Mining Code — are still ongoing and actively contested.

🌍
Why this matters for Africa: Many African nations are ISA member states. The regulatory decisions being made right now — about environmental standards, benefit-sharing, technology transfer, and who gets to mine — will affect Africa's ocean future for decades. African countries need technical expertise and institutional presence in these negotiations.
An ISA Council session where member states negotiate the Mining Code — the regulatory framework that will govern commercial deep sea mining. African nations with institutional presence in these negotiations are better positioned to shape outcomes that serve their interests.

Why Should African Coastal Communities Care?

For many coastal communities, deep sea mining may seem like a distant issue happening far from shore. However, the discussions being held now about ocean resources and blue economy development will have long-term impacts on Africa's coastal future — regardless of whether commercial mining ever reaches African waters.

Africa has one of the world's most strategically important coastlines. The continent is bordered by the Atlantic Ocean, the Indian Ocean, the Mediterranean Sea, and the Red Sea. Coastal economies support tens of millions of people through fishing, tourism, ports, shipping, marine trade, and maritime transport.

East Africa's coastal communities have depended on the Indian Ocean for generations. The emerging blue economy — including ocean governance, marine technology, and offshore industries — will shape the region's maritime future.
Coastal fishing communities across East Africa are the most directly affected by shifts in ocean governance — yet they are often the least represented in international negotiations. Inclusive policy-making is essential to ensuring the blue economy works for people, not only for industry.

As global interest in ocean resources grows, African countries are increasingly being drawn into discussions related to ocean governance, marine scientific research, underwater technology, seabed mapping, marine environmental management, and blue economy investment. In East Africa especially, the Indian Ocean continues to grow in geopolitical and economic importance. Ports such as Mombasa, Dar es Salaam, Maputo, and Durban play critical roles in international trade and maritime connectivity.

Future ocean industries could create significant new opportunities in marine engineering, oceanographic research, environmental science, maritime law, offshore operations, underwater robotics, and ocean technology — fields where African professionals are currently underrepresented but where demand is growing rapidly.

Possible Benefits for Africa and Coastal Communities

Although deep sea mining remains a contested topic, discussions around the sector have created meaningful conversations about how Africa can strengthen its participation in ocean science and marine industries — regardless of the ultimate trajectory of commercial seabed mining.

Africa's ocean economy creates demand for professionals across marine engineering, oceanography, maritime law, underwater robotics, and environmental science — fields where investment in African education is urgently needed.

Education and capacity building represent one of the most tangible near-term benefits. African students, scientists, engineers, and maritime professionals are increasingly participating in international training programmes related to ocean governance, marine science, and seabed exploration. Such capacity building helps African countries develop sovereign expertise in marine engineering, oceanography, environmental monitoring, hydrography, underwater robotics, maritime policy, and offshore technology.

Scientific development is another avenue. Deep ocean research requires advanced vessels, laboratories, marine equipment, data analysis systems, and specialised training — encouraging investment in marine research institutions and universities. Improved ocean knowledge also supports fisheries management, marine conservation, coastal planning, climate research, and maritime safety.

For African youth specifically, the growth of ocean industries represents an opportunity to build careers in sectors that have historically received limited attention and investment on the continent. This is why building ocean knowledge now — ahead of commercial demand — is a strategic priority.

Environmental Concerns and Community Questions

Despite the potential opportunities, deep sea mining raises serious environmental questions that scientists are still actively working to understand. The deep ocean remains one of the least explored environments on Earth — researchers continue discovering new marine species in these regions, and the ecological dynamics of deep ocean systems are incompletely understood.

Scientific and environmental concerns include destruction of seabed habitats, impacts on marine biodiversity, sediment plumes dispersing through the water column, underwater noise pollution, and potential effects on marine food chains that could eventually reach coastal fisheries. Fishing communities across East Africa are particularly interested in understanding whether deep ocean disturbance could indirectly affect fish populations and the coastal marine ecosystems upon which their livelihoods depend.

At present, there is ongoing scientific debate about the scale and permanence of these environmental impacts. Many experts are calling for stronger precautionary research before any commercial mining begins. Environmental protection has consequently become a central — and contested — pillar of ISA negotiations and broader international ocean governance discussions.

Why Public Awareness Matters

One of the most significant challenges surrounding deep sea mining in Africa is limited public awareness. Many ordinary citizens — and even many government officials — have never encountered terms like the ISA, seabed minerals, ocean governance, or deep sea mining. Yet decisions being made today about how ocean resources will be governed, regulated, and distributed could influence African communities for generations.

Coastal communities should have access to clear, understandable information about emerging ocean industries. Awareness enables communities to participate in national and international discussions, ask informed questions of their governments, understand the environmental and economic stakes, and advocate for transparent and equitable governance of shared ocean resources.

"Ocean-related decisions should not only involve governments, scientists, and international organisations. Communities that depend on the ocean for their livelihoods deserve to understand what is happening — and to have a voice in what happens next."

Cyril Kariuki — Founder, Bahari BlueTech

Africa Must Build Ocean Knowledge and Expertise

As global interest in the ocean continues to grow, Africa cannot afford to remain a passive observer. The continent has enormous — and largely untapped — potential to develop expertise in marine engineering, ocean science, offshore technology, maritime law, underwater robotics, marine environmental protection, and ocean data management.

African universities, maritime academies, and research institutions have a critical role to play in training the next generation of ocean professionals. Investing in ocean knowledge is not only important for deep sea mining discussions — it is foundational to fisheries management, maritime transport, climate resilience, coastal protection, renewable ocean energy, and sustainable blue economy development more broadly.

Africa's future in ocean industries should not be defined solely by resource extraction. It should be shaped by knowledge, innovation, research, environmental responsibility, and genuine local participation. Countries that invest in scientific and technical ocean capacity now will be better positioned — and better protected — when commercial decisions about the seabed are finally made.

Conclusion

Deep sea mining is becoming one of the most important — and most contested — ocean governance questions of the modern era. While the industry is still developing, the global conversations about seabed minerals, clean energy supply chains, and international maritime governance are accelerating rapidly.

For African coastal communities, engagement with these discussions is not optional — it is strategic. The future of the blue economy will not only involve fishing and shipping, but science, technology, environmental protection, and ocean governance. Whether deep sea mining becomes beneficial or harmful for Africa will depend on scientific research, environmental responsibility, strong and transparent governance, and how meaningfully communities are included in conversations about the future of the resources that lie beneath their ocean.

As Africa continues building its blue economy, coastal communities should not be left behind. Awareness, education, and participation are not just aspirational — they are essential to ensuring that future ocean development serves both people and the marine environment upon which millions still depend.

Frequently Asked Questions
Is deep sea mining already happening in Africa?
Not yet on a large commercial scale. Current activities mainly involve scientific research and exploration rather than full industrial mining. Countries, scientists, and international organisations are still studying the deep ocean and negotiating the rules that would govern future mining. However, global interest is growing rapidly — meaning Africa should begin preparing now through education, policy engagement, marine research, and institutional capacity building.
Why are countries suddenly interested in the deep ocean?
The world is moving rapidly toward cleaner energy technologies — electric vehicles, renewable energy systems, and large battery storage. These technologies require minerals like nickel, cobalt, copper, and manganese. Some of these minerals are believed to exist in enormous quantities on the deep ocean floor, making the seabed an area of growing global strategic interest.
Could deep sea mining affect fishermen and coastal livelihoods?
This is one of the most important questions currently being studied. Scientists are still researching how deep sea mining could affect marine ecosystems and whether impacts could eventually influence fish populations or ocean food chains closer to coastal areas. Many fishing communities are calling for careful scientific research and strong environmental protections before any commercial mining begins.
What is the International Seabed Authority (ISA)?
The ISA is the global organisation responsible for managing mineral-related activities in international seabed areas beyond national borders. Established under UNCLOS, its role includes developing mining regulations, issuing exploration contracts, promoting scientific research, and establishing environmental protection measures for the deep seabed.
Does the deep ocean really belong to everyone?
Under international law, deep seabed areas beyond national jurisdiction are considered the "Common Heritage of Mankind." This legal principle means these resources should be managed for the benefit of all humanity — not controlled by a few countries or corporations. This principle is enshrined in UNCLOS and is the legal foundation of the ISA system.
Can Africa benefit from deep sea mining even without mining itself?
Yes. Benefits can also come through marine science education, technical training, ocean research, maritime industries, port services, marine engineering, environmental monitoring, offshore support operations, and blue economy investment. Africa does not need to wait for commercial mining to begin before strengthening its ocean expertise and institutional participation in global maritime governance.
What is the biggest challenge Africa faces regarding deep sea mining?
Limited awareness and technical capacity. Many African countries are still developing expertise in ocean science, marine technology, and seabed governance. Without strong local knowledge and active institutional participation, Africa risks becoming a passive observer in decisions about future ocean industries — with rules written by others, in interests that may not align with Africa's communities.
Could deep sea mining create opportunities for African youth?
Potentially yes. As ocean industries expand, demand is growing for professionals in marine engineering, oceanography, hydrography, underwater robotics, maritime law, environmental science, offshore operations, GIS and ocean mapping, and marine technology. This is why investing in ocean education and maritime training in African universities and academies is a strategic priority.
